# Struck Grid

*A design philosophy*

---

## The movement

Order is laid down first, and then it is claimed. Struck Grid begins with a
system so exact that it disappears — a lattice of whole units, every measure a
multiple of the last, nothing arbitrary, nothing approximate — and then permits
exactly one gesture to cross it. The grid is the argument; the mark is the
verdict. Everything in this movement lives in the tension between a structure
that could have been drawn by a machine and a stroke that could only have been
made by a hand that knew precisely where to put it. Neither half is decoration.
Remove the system and the gesture is noise; remove the gesture and the system is
furniture. The work is the collision, held at the exact moment of impact, and
holding it there is the labour: it is the product of countless hours of moving a
single edge by fractions of a unit until the collision reads as inevitable
rather than arranged.

## Space and form

Form is orthogonal, closed, and heavy. Rectangles meet at right angles or they
do not meet at all; there are no bevels, no radii softening a corner into an
apology, no shapes that could not be cut from board with a blade and a steel
rule. Weight is distributed like masonry — the eye should feel that these
elements have mass and would be difficult to lift. Negative space is not
leftover; it is cut, deliberately, and it carries as much of the composition as
the marks do. A void in this system is a decision, sized to the same unit as
everything around it, and its proportions are refined with the same painstaking
attention given to the objects it separates. The single permitted exception to
all this rectitude is the overshoot: one element, and only one, is allowed to
run past the boundary it belongs to. That overshoot is the whole personality of
the movement, and its length is the most laboured-over dimension in the work.

## Colour and material

The palette is closed and small enough to hold in one hand: a ground, an ink,
and one chromatic voice that is never used for beauty. Colour here does not
describe or decorate — it *selects*. Where the chromatic voice falls, that thing
matters; where it does not fall, that thing is context. This makes colour a
grammar rather than a mood, and grammar is unforgiving: a second decorative hue
would make the first one meaningless, so there is never a second. The ground is
paper, not screen — matte, printed, slightly warm of true neutral, the colour of
something that exists physically and can be marked on. The ink is not black but
nearly so, because true black belongs to voids and this ink belongs to objects.
Shadows, where they appear, are flat opaque offsets — never blur, never
gradient, never light. They are not illumination; they are a second silhouette,
proof that the object is a physical thing sitting slightly above its ground.

## Scale and rhythm

Scale is used at its extremes and almost nowhere between. There is the
monumental — a form so large it becomes architecture, so large the eye reads it
as surface before it reads it as sign — and there is the clinical: small,
uppercase, wide-tracked annotation set at the size of a specimen label. The
absence of the comfortable middle is what gives the work its charge, and the
discipline to refuse that middle register is the mark of expertise. Rhythm comes
from repetition at a fixed interval, and from the one place where that interval
is broken. Set a rule twelve times at an identical measure and the thirteenth
displacement becomes an event. This is patient work: the accumulation must be
genuinely regular, drawn with master-level consistency, or the break reads as an
error instead of a statement.

## Typography

Letterforms are grotesque, drawn tight — tracking pulled negative until the
counters just hold, so that a word becomes a single dense object with one
silhouette rather than a row of separate characters. Type is never typeset onto
the composition; it is built into it, occupying the grid on the same terms as
every other form, aligned to the same units, carrying the same mass. Words are
scarce and load-bearing. One word, sized enormous, will say more than a sentence
ever could, and the small annotations that surround it exist to establish that
this is a considered artefact rather than a poster — reference marks, measures,
coordinates, the vocabulary of something that has been *studied*. Every terminal
is checked, every optical relationship corrected by eye after the mathematics is
satisfied, because mathematics gets a form to almost-right and only a
practitioner's judgement carries it the rest of the way.

## The standard

Nothing in Struck Grid may be approximate. Every edge lands on the lattice or
deliberately, measurably departs from it; every departure is a decision that can
be defended in units. The finished work must look as though it was drawn by
someone at the absolute top of their field who returned to it many times — an
artefact with the density of something laboured over across weeks, where a
viewer who looks closely finds that the details reward the looking, and a viewer
who glances simply registers authority. It should feel less designed than
*resolved*: the composition that was always going to be correct, found by
someone patient and exacting enough to keep working until it appeared.
